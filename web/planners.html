For planners

What planners can do with this

Skimline answers one planning question from open data: where can water-scooping aircraft refill, how fast can they work from there, and which of those refill sites disappear in a dry year. It is for decisions made before the fire season. It is not for flight operations.

1. Find where scoopers work slowly

The decision. Where to rely on scoopers, and where to plan for helicopters, land-based tankers or ground crews instead.

What the site shows. The coloured surface is minutes per load. Blue means fifteen or more loads an hour from one aircraft; red means fewer than five. The third summary box gives the share of everything burned since 2016 that lay more than nine minutes from usable water.

How. Pick the region. Set the run length for your aircraft and elevation. Look for red and orange ground that overlaps past fire perimeters. Click it for the exact figures.

The analytics you get. Minutes per load, loads an hour and litres an hour at any point; the share of the area within six minutes; the share of burned area beyond nine.

2. See which reservoirs must stay usable

The decision. Which reservoirs to protect from deep drawdown in fire season, and what is at stake if they are not.

What the site shows. For each observed reservoir, a chart of its usable run every clear month since 2021, measured on satellite images. Red dots mark months below the run required. "Driest month seen" recalculates the whole map with each reservoir at its lowest.

How. Open "Reservoirs seen from space". The list is ordered by how often each reservoir fell short. Click one to see it. Then switch between "As mapped" and "Driest month seen" and compare the summary boxes.

The analytics you get. Months below the required run, the shortest run and when it occurred, how far the water surface shrank, and the change in usable sites and covered area between a full and a dry state.

Read it with care. A long enough run does not mean a usable reservoir. In April 2023 La Baells in Catalonia was closed to scooper aircraft with its water surface at 42 % of the mapped outline, although its straight run was still long enough on paper and, by our depth check, deep enough. What it lacked was width: with a 200 m clear corridor its run that day fell well short. Treat a sharp fall in water surface as a warning, and confirm with the reservoir operator.

Where a gauge is connected (Catalonia so far), each reservoir's card also shows the water level below which its run was lost, how much of the run is known to be at least 1.8 m deep, and how full it has been every month since 2000. The level is the number a dam operator can act on.

3. Find where a new water point would help

The decision. Where to designate, build or maintain a water source for aircraft.

What the site shows. Areas that are both far from usable water and have burned. The list of large fires furthest from water points to them directly.

How. Use the fire list to jump to the slowest large fires. Note the minutes per load and the nearest water. Lower the run-length slider to see whether a shorter-run aircraft would have had closer water.

Coming. An automatic ranking of locations by how much burned-area exposure a new water point would bring within six minutes.

4. Brief crews from elsewhere

The decision. What to tell aircraft and crews arriving from another province or country.

What the site shows. The same measurement everywhere, with each water body's longest run and its compass direction. It is a first orientation, to be confirmed with the local agency's own approved sites.

5. Support reviews and budget cases

The decision. How to explain why aerial attack was slow in a past fire, or why an investment is needed.

What the site shows. For each large fire since 2016: its size, the nearest usable water, the distance and the minutes per load. Every figure traces to a public source listed on the methods page, so it can be cited.

What it adds to an agency's own list

  • Time. A list says a reservoir is usable. Satellite images say whether it was usable that month.
  • Consistency. One method across jurisdictions, so regions can be compared.
  • Openness. The data, code and assumptions are public, so partners and the public can see the same picture.
  • A link to outcomes. Water access is set against where fires actually burned.

What it will not do

  • It does not know depth, submerged hazards, power lines or boats.
  • It does not check terrain on the approach or climb-out.
  • It does not know which sites an agency has approved.
  • It assumes straight-line flight on flat terrain.
  • It covers scoopers only, not helicopters or smaller aircraft.

Treat every result as a prompt for a closer look by people who know the ground.

Coverage

The region list on the map shows what is built and what is planned. Every region runs through the same pipeline, so adding one is a matter of naming its area and running five steps. Planned regions were chosen for large, well-documented recent fires. The aim is to choose future regions from burned-area data, starting with the places that have burned most.

If you work in a region you would like covered, or can say what this gets wrong for your area, that is the most useful feedback there is.
