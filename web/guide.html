User guide

How to use this map

Skimline shows where water-scooping firefighting aircraft can refill, how long each load takes from any point, and how reservoirs lose their usable water in dry months. It is a planning aid built from open data. It is not for flight operations.

▶ Watch the recorded tour on the home page: about a minute, no clicking. On the map itself, "Guide me, step by step" asks what you want to find out and walks you to the answer. Planners can go straight to what the site helps you decide.

In one minute

  1. Pick a region from the list.
  2. Read the colours. Blue ground is close to usable water, so an aircraft can deliver many loads an hour. Orange and red ground is far from it.
  3. Click any spot to get its numbers: the nearest usable water, minutes per load, loads an hour and litres an hour.
  4. Switch to "Latest image" or "Driest month" (where reservoirs have been observed) to see them as they last looked, or at their lowest.

The controls

The guided tour

The blue button at the top of the panel opens the tour. It starts by asking what you want to find out:

  • How fast could water bombers work at a place I care about? You pick a place and end with minutes per load, loads an hour and the position of the nearest usable water.
  • Which reservoirs let us down in a dry year? You end with the reservoirs that lost their usable run, when, and what it cost in covered ground. Offered where reservoirs have been measured from space.
  • Where did past fires burn far from usable water? You end at the slowest large fire, with its nearest water, and see whether a shorter-run aircraft would have had closer water.
  • Just show me around. A walk through every part of the page.

Each step explains one thing. Where a step needs an action, a "Your turn" box tells you what to click, and the tour waits until you have done it. If you are unsure, "Show me" does it for you. Nothing moves on a timer, so take as long as you like. Back returns to the previous step, Esc closes the tour, and the map stays as you left it so you can carry on from your result. Adding ?tour=1 to the map's address opens it on arrival.

Region

The list is grouped by country. Regions marked "planned" are queued for the same method and cannot be opened yet. The address in your browser always names the region on screen, so you can share a link to it. The dashed box is the area analysed. Results outside it are not shown, because water beyond the downloaded area is unknown.

Water run the aircraft needs

A scooper needs a long, straight stretch of open water. The slider sets how long. Three presets match published figures:

  • 1 mile: US Forest Service figure at sea level.
  • 1 nautical mile (default): the screening minimum used by Natural Resources Canada authors.
  • 2.5 miles: US Forest Service figure at 8,000 ft. Use a longer run for high lakes and hot days.

As you move the slider, water bodies that are too short turn grey and the whole map recalculates.

Clear width of water

Below the slider, choose how wide the open water must be along that run: 100, 150, 200, 250 or 300 m. It starts at 100 m, close to the published minimum. A reservoir that has been drawn down can stay long while becoming too narrow, so a wider setting is the more cautious test in a dry year. The reservoir charts, the dry-year view and every figure on the page follow the width you choose.

As mapped, latest image, or driest month

This appears where reservoirs have been measured from satellite images. "As mapped" uses each reservoir's mapped outline, which is usually its full level. "Latest image" shows each observed reservoir as it last looked from space, and says which months those images are from. "Driest month" puts each observed reservoir at its lowest measured month since 2021. The summary boxes then show the old value struck through beside the new one. The reservoirs did not all reach their lowest in the same month, so read this as a stress test, not as a picture of one date.

The three summary boxes

  • Usable refill sites: water bodies with a run at least as long as the slider.
  • Area within 6 min per load: the share of the analysed area where an aircraft can deliver ten or more loads an hour.
  • Burned area over 9 min per load: of everything that burned in fires of 100 ha or more since 2016, the share that was far from usable water.

Layers

Three tick boxes switch the coloured surface, the fire perimeters and the too-short water bodies on and off. Zoom in to see a yellow dashed line on each usable water body: that is its longest straight run.

Reading a spot

Click anywhere inside the dashed box. A line is drawn to the nearest usable run and a card shows:

  • Minutes per load: the round trip to the water and back, from a US Forest Service rule of thumb for flat terrain.
  • Loads an hour and litres an hour for one aircraft with a 6,137-litre tank.
  • Coordinates: the latitude and longitude of the nearest point on that water's run, and of the spot you clicked, in decimal degrees (WGS 84). Click a coordinate to select it for copying.

Reading a water body

Click a lake or reservoir. The card shows its longest straight run, the compass direction of that run, its mapped area, and the latitude and longitude of both ends of the run. Where the reservoir has been observed from space, a small chart shows its usable run month by month. The dashed line on the chart is the run required; red dots are months that fell below it. The card also gives the lowest share of the full area that was water. A sharp fall in that share is a warning even when the run still looks long enough, because the water that is left is narrower.

Where an official gauge is connected, the card adds three things: the water level below which the run was lost, how much of the run is known to be at least 1.8 m deep, and how full the reservoir has been every month since 2000.

The two lists

  • Reservoirs seen from space ranks observed reservoirs by how often they dropped below the required run. Click one to go to it.
  • Large fires furthest from usable water lists fires of 500 ha or more with the slowest loads. Click one to see it on the map.

Questions it can help answer

If you are Try this
A fire agency planner Find the red areas that have also burned. These are places where scoopers work slowly and other aircraft or a designated water point may be needed.
A reservoir or basin manager Open "Reservoirs seen from space" and find the ones that lose their run in dry months. Switch to "Driest month" to see how much ground depends on them.
A municipality Click your town or the forest beside it. Compare "As mapped" with "Driest month".
A crew working outside its home region Use the map for a first briefing on where long water is, then confirm with the local agency.
A researcher or student Read the Methods page. Every number carries its source, and the code is open.

What it does not tell you

  • Whether the water is deep enough, or free of power lines, boats and submerged hazards.
  • Whether terrain allows the approach and climb-out.
  • Whether a site is approved for use.
  • Anything about helicopters or smaller aircraft, which need far less water.

Full detail is on the Methods, sources and limits page.

Words used

  • Scooper: an aircraft that refills by skimming the water surface, such as the Canadair CL-415.
  • Run: the longest straight stretch of open water in a lake or reservoir, with clear water on both sides.
  • Clear width: how wide that open water must be along the whole run. You set it in step 2.
  • Load: one tank of water dropped on the fire.
  • Drawn down: a reservoir whose level has been lowered by use or drought.
  • Share of full area: a map draws a reservoir at the size it is when full. This is how much of that shape was still water on a given satellite image. 92% is nearly full; 42% means more than half the surface had become dry bank.
  • Water level: the height of the water surface above sea level, from the reservoir's official gauge. Shown where a gauge is connected, with the percent of capacity that day.
  • Known deep: water that was still there when the level stood at least 1.8 m lower, so it is at least 1.8 m deep now. Near the lowest level on record this cannot be known.
